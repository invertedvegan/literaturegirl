I Like That My Device Does Less

(in support of software minimalism)

2026/11/11

The first time I used any form of Linux distribution was whenever I was thirteen. At the time, I wanted to find the software setup that would allow me to continue to 'do the most things' — essentially, I wanted my desktop to operate just like Windows had, and for every piece of software I previously used (or the FOSS counterpart I now had installed) to continue to run seamlessly. I wanted it to continue to be the distraction-inducing machine I had always utilised it as.

Since then, I've come to change my perspective a lot on what I actually want my devices to be capable of doing. I have come to learn that I am the kind of person (as I'm sure many others are) who cannot seem to help indulging in distractions once they are made accessible to me. On my devices, the culprits usually involved short-form content via social media applications, endless reams of music and television shows, and instant messaging applications. Heck, I even found myself habitually scrolling through my old emails and my weather application once I removed all of the instant gratification oriented applications from my devices.

That is to say: in order to prevent myself from wasting my time with unnecessary distractions and overstimulating myself as a result, I seemed to need a device that simply did less. Something that wouldn't allow me to habitually access these distractions in a singular tap of a button, even if I wanted to. Something that wasn't trying to do everything or be everything all at once. Something much more intentional than that. If I were to interact with media, I knew I wanted (needed) it to be far more thoughtful and intentional if I were to prevent the feeling of my time and my intelligence slipping away from me before I even realised it was happening.


There's something about having to type in a command to navigate to a specific file or application that forces me to be more intentional about the software I am choosing to interact with on my device. This stands in stark contrast to how mindlessly I am inclined to behave whenever I simply click on buttons on a standard 'user-friendly' GUI. I'm convinced that a lot of the devices and software that are pushed onto people continually are designed to be both mind-numbing and time-wasting: they are merely 'tools', yes, but they remain tools that are often malicious in design, and that latter point matters immensely.

Upon trying different distributions and applications, and also learning about different software philosophies, I've come to the conclusion that the less my device is able to offer me in regards to functionality is often, on the whole, better. Obviously, there are certain basic tasks I need to be able to complete (file management, website maintenance, emailing, voice calling, listening to music), but I now seek to complete these in the simplest and most intentional way possible.

In lieu of streaming platforms for music, and more GUI-intensive music players such as MusicBee, I've discovered I actually enjoy listening to my music more on a TUI-based application such as cmus. Whenever I create or download files, I am more likely to organise them neatly using something like yazi (or mere terminal commands) than I ever was whenever I used a GUI file manager. In the absence of multiple instant messaging platforms, I find far more fulfilling conversation in the longer-form email format.


Wendell Berry once wrote a short essay entitled 'Why I Am Not Going to Buy a Computer', in which he wrote the following, which I think about often:

"I disbelieve, and therefore strongly resent, the assertion that I or anybody else could write better or more easily with a computer than with a pencil. I do not see why I should not be as scientific about this as the next fellow: when somebody has used a computer to write work that is demonstrably better than Dante's, and when this better is demonstrably attributable to the use of a computer, then I will speak of computers with a more respectful tone of voice[.]"

There seems to be a huge focus within our current zeitgeist on some vague sense of progress. 'Progress = good' is a common mantra. Is it really always so good, though? A lot of the time we don't even seem to know what we want to progress towards. We just seem to know we must keep moving, even if we don't know where we are going. A huge way in which I observe people doing this is through increasingly complex technology that, if anything, we do not quite seem designed to handle or yet ready for.

Whilst a fountain pen or pencil may require more maintenance than a keyboard, and it may appear inconvenient to practice handwriting or to procure writing paper, to avoid the smudging of ink, and so on, there are also certain qualities to the act of writing something by hand (perhaps these very 'issues' themselves) that encourage the writer to take more time in the act of writing, to be more deliberate, to think more. These inconveniences are not inherently negative or unhelpful things. In some ways, they may push us in a more positive direction, even if it may not seem obvious at first.

I now apply a very similar mindset to the technology I interact with in general. What may at first seem foreign, unintuitive, or even lacking in features, often quickly reveals itself as truly user-respecting. Far from assaulting the senses, these applications instead cater to a specific need simply and intentionally, without any fluff — they are solution-solving with no ulterior motives, straightforward and respecting of the user's time. Isn't that exactly what software ought to be?
